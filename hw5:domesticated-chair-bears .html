page 2: the domesticating of the chair bear

years after the expedition returned east, secret rumors spread among frontier carpenters about how to pacify the strange creatures. a few brave settlers discovered that if you left an old, splintered rocking chair on your porch, a chair bear would guard your home for weeks just to earn the right to eat the armrests.
they quickly became the unofficial mascot of wilderness woodworkers, who would trade their failed carpentry projects to the bears in exchange for heavy logs dragged out of the deep woods.
however, keeping a chair bear near a civilized town proved nearly impossible. a single escaped bear once wandered into a fine dining hall in st. louis, causing thousands of dollars in damages not by biting the guests, but by systematically consuming twenty-four matching chippendale dining chairs in under an hour.

by the 1840s, a few eccentric wealthy landowners attempted to breed the bears as domestic status symbols. they built specialized 'forage parlors' filled with cheap, poorly constructed pine stools to keep the animals entertained and away from the expensive family heirlooms.
this gave rise to the short-lived frontier profession of the 'chair-bear wrangler,' individuals hired specifically to inspect furniture stores for bear-proofing. a good wrangler knew that rubbing a table with bitter onion juice could deter a juvenile bear, but nothing short of an iron vault could save a master-crafted cherrywood grandfather clock from an adult bull.
despite these efforts, true domestication never succeeded because the bears' tastes evolved alongside human fashion. when upholstered furniture became popular in the late Victorian era, the chair bears experienced widespread indigestion from attempting to eat velvet tassels and brass upholstery tacks. the creatures eventually retreated deeper into the Pacific Northwest forests, where it is rumored they still live today, occasionally raiding remote logging cabins not for the food inside, but for the log cabins themselves.
